[Our opening section](/posts/2-why-hegel-is-so-hard-to-get-right) mentioned McGowan's claim that Hegel arrived a century ahead of the vocabulary he needed, in [Sigmund Freud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmund_Freud)'s account of the unconscious. It's time to unpack this.

Hegel's central claim — that subjects do not seek to eliminate contradiction but to sustain and deepen it — had no comfortable home in the philosophical language available to him. Every tradition before Freud assumed, more or less, that people strive toward harmony and self-identity, and even philosophers who acknowledged that this goal is never fully reached still treated it as the goal worth reaching. Freud's most important contribution, on McGowan's reading, is the discovery that **subjects do not actually seek their own good, regardless of what they consciously believe about themselves.** The unconscious drives a person to act against their own stated intentions, in ways the person cannot consciously trace, which means the subject is structurally divided against itself in a way no amount of willpower or self-reflection can repair. Put side by side with Hegel's claim, the parallel is exact: for Hegel, no identity can simply be itself, because it is always internally divided by what it is not; for Freud, no subject can simply be what it consciously intends to be, because it is always being undermined from within by a desire it does not control. The structure is identical in both cases. Freud simply has plain, clinical language for it that Hegel's own century had not yet developed.

The single most useful piece of that vocabulary comes from a distinction Freud draws in his 1915 paper "Instincts and Their Vicissitudes," between a drive's **aim** and its **object**. Ordinary intuition assumes the aim of any drive is simply to obtain its object — you want something specific, and getting it is the whole point. Freud's argument is that this gets the relationship exactly backward. **The aim of a drive is satisfaction. The object is only the vehicle through which the drive pursues that satisfaction, and the drive is fundamentally indifferent to which particular vehicle it happens to be using.** The object changes constantly across a lifetime; the aim underneath it never does. And crucially, satisfaction was never coming from finally obtaining the object, it was coming from the process of pursuing it the whole time.

This explains something almost everyone recognizes but rarely examines. A person who trades in a perfectly good car for a new model every year is not simply gullible or wasteful. They genuinely believe, each time, that satisfaction is waiting inside the new object. It never quite is. A few months later the same restlessness returns, and a new object gets recruited to carry the same craving forward. A more everyday version of the identical structure shows up in something as small as compulsively checking a phone for new notifications: the satisfaction was never really in the notification itself, since it evaporates the instant it's read, and the urge to check again typically arrives within minutes. In both cases the object was never the point. The point was the movement of desire, which the absence of any final, fully satisfying object is precisely what makes possible in the first place. Chase the object down and get it, and the restlessness doesn't end, it simply migrates to something else.

In 1920, with [_Beyond the Pleasure Principle_](https://www.librarything.com/work/29167/t/Beyond-the-Pleasure-Principle), Freud makes a revision that McGowan treats as the moment his theory fully aligns with Hegel's. Earlier in his career, Freud had located the trouble in the _object_ — specifically the forbidden, incestuous object a subject has to repress and replace with something socially acceptable, which at least held out the theoretical hope that psychoanalysis could offer real relief by helping a person come to terms with the true nature of their own desire. After 1920, Freud sees something harder to sit with. The problem was never really the object. **The problem is the aim itself.** The subject achieves satisfaction _through the failure_ to attain what it consciously wants, and it engineers that failure without ever being able to admit as much to itself — because consciously knowing you are sabotaging your own goals would immediately dissolve the mechanism that makes the sabotage work in the first place.

McGowan's clearest illustration of this involves parents who sacrifice everything for a child's future: years of disciplined deprivation for a payoff that arrives, if it ever arrives at all, decades later. The unconscious uses the mere _image_ of that eventual reward to license present satisfaction in the sacrifice itself, which means the parents are, without ever consciously realizing it, more satisfied by the years of struggle than the grown child will ever be by whatever comfortable life that struggle eventually purchases. The future payoff functions as a kind of bribe offered to consciousness, a story consciousness can accept as its reason for acting. The actual satisfaction, meanwhile, lives in the sustained contradiction between present deprivation and future promise — a contradiction the subject has no real interest in ever seeing resolved, however sincerely it complains about the sacrifice out loud.

This gives our portrayal of the [Absolute](/posts/11-the-absolute-and-reconciliation) a psychoanalytic double, and makes it concrete rather than mystical. The Absolute is not the moment a subject finally achieves total knowledge; it is the moment the subject **runs out of objects** — every available route to resolving the underlying contradiction has been tried and exhausted, and the hope of some future solution simply evaporates. What remains, once that hope is gone, is contradiction itself, with nothing left to pretend a solution is still waiting somewhere up ahead. [Jacques Lacan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Lacan)'s notion of _traversing the fantasy_ names the identical event from inside actual psychoanalytic treatment: a patient reaches the point of recognizing there was never an object beyond the fantasy that could finally deliver satisfaction, that satisfaction had been coming from the structure of desiring itself the whole time, not from any imaginable fulfillment of it. This is not resignation, since resignation would mean giving up on wanting anything. Traversing the fantasy is closer to finally recognizing, honestly, what desire had actually been doing all along.

McGowan reaches for a film to make all of this concrete: [_The Bridge on the River Kwai_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bridge_on_the_River_Kwai), in which a British prisoner of war, Colonel Nicholson, oversees the construction of a bridge for his Japanese captors, taking real pride in his own workmanship, only to have his own side send commandos to blow it up. Nicholson initially resists the sabotage — the bridge is his creation, the one thing giving his imprisonment any dignity — but in the film's final moment he falls onto the detonator and destroys it himself as he dies. This is the Absolute enacted rather than merely described: the contradiction between the pride of building something well and the demands of the war he was actually fighting was never going to resolve into some clean, comfortable answer. Nicholson does not escape that contradiction. He inhabits it completely, which is the only way such a contradiction can ever actually be inhabited.
