This section covers a lot of ground — how authority actually works, two competing definitions of freedom, one of Hegel's most abused phrases, three historical epochs, a serious disagreement about how his system ends, an honest look at where Hegel personally failed his own philosophy, and a final question about why history doesn't feel finished. It helps to take each piece on its own before connecting them.

**Why authority collapses once contradiction is recognized.** Most people encounter Hegel's political philosophy through the [_Philosophy of History_](https://www.librarything.com/work/9810/t/The-Philosophy-of-History), and this is, on McGowan's account, close to the worst possible entry point. Terms like "world-historical individual" appear nowhere else in Hegel's system; the book was delivered as lectures to students who already had the whole apparatus of the [_Science of Logic_](https://www.librarything.com/work/2383615/t/Hegels-Science-of-Logic) in hand, and, read on its own, it can look like a straightforward, ethnocentric claim that history marches teleologically toward Christian European freedom, sacrificing whoever stands in the way along the road. The corrective is to read it as a direct application of the logic already established earlier in this guide: [**authority depends on consistent self-identity.**](/posts/9-freedom-as-self-determination) People submit to a ruler, a god, a natural order, or a market only for as long as they believe that authority stands somewhere above contradiction — undivided, self-sufficient, beyond the reach of the doubts that afflict everything else. The moment Reason reveals that no being escapes contradiction, that the ruler, the divine, and the market are all secretly split against themselves exactly the way everything else in this guide has been, every such authority loses its grip, because there is no longer anything qualitatively different separating the person who rules from the people who are ruled.

**Two different definitions of freedom.** This produces a distinction from the earlier sections worth restating in explicitly political terms, because the gap between the two definitions is exactly where a great deal of modern political disagreement actually lives. **Liberal freedom** is the absence of external constraint: you're free when no one is actively stopping you from acting. **Hegelian freedom** is the absence of _impressive_ external substances: you're free when you no longer treat any authority as self-identical and beyond question. The liberal definition misses how the most effective domination actually operates, because direct constraint is the _weakest_ form of power — it can be openly resisted, named, and organized against. The stronger, quieter form of domination works by getting the subject to genuinely believe in the consistency of an authority, so the subject voluntarily organizes an entire life around it, no coercion required. A person living inside a market economy can feel completely free — free to work wherever, buy whatever, invest however — while believing, without ever seriously questioning it, in the market as a perfect, self-regulating, almost natural force that simply exists the way weather exists. That unexamined belief in the market's internal consistency is the unfreedom liberalism structurally cannot see, because liberalism only ever checks for the overt kind of constraint someone could point at and name.

Lets reframe what Hegel calls the **cunning of reason**, probably the single most abused phrase in his entire political vocabulary. The claim, in Hegel's own words, is that reason "sets the passions to work for itself," sacrificing individuals to advance a larger historical purpose. Read carelessly, this sounds genuinely monstrous — history using people as disposable tools, their suffering justified afterward as the necessary cost of progress. Reading the phrase back through the [_Science of Logic_](https://www.librarything.com/work/2383615/t/Hegels-Science-of-Logic)'s discussion of teleology reverses the relationship entirely. Subjects pursue their own private ends, and in doing so they inevitably have to use _means_ — tools, shared institutions, public infrastructure — and it is in those means, not in any private end, that the universal actually shows up in the world. Reason is not a puppeteer manipulating subjects from above, pulling strings for purposes they don't share. Reason is the medium subjects themselves use whenever they act on their own private purposes at all. Hegel's own example is the plough, which he calls more honorable than the harvest it produces, because the means outlasts and exceeds the particular end that first motivated its use. A contemporary version of the same point: riding public transit is, for any given passenger, entirely about a private errand — getting from home to work, nothing more. But in using it you constitute, alongside a car full of total strangers, a shared public world, a bond that turns out to matter more, in the long run, than the private errand that put you on the train in the first place. Reason "using" you, in this reframed sense, just means: acting on your own purposes always drags a public world into existence alongside them, whether you intended that or not.

**Three historical epochs, one expanding recognition.** The three historical epochs Hegel sketches follow from this logic rather than from any claim about racial destiny. In the **Asiatic world**, only the ruler is free, acting without deference to any higher authority above himself. In the **Greek and Roman world**, an entire class of free men shares that position, while slaves, women, and non-citizens remain excluded from it. In the **modern world**, all subjects can in principle recognize that no authority escapes contradiction, and therefore no one holds a natural right to rule over anyone else simply by birth. This is not a cultural preference for Europe dressed up as metaphysics. It is a claim about the gradual, historical spread of one specific recognition — which is also why [Alexandre Kojève](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandre_Koj%C3%A8ve)'s hugely influential twentieth-century reading of Hegel, though it correctly identifies the "end of history" as central to the system, gets the ending wrong in a crucial way. Kojève locates the end of history in the _elimination_ of contradiction through a society of total mutual recognition, a state in which everyone finally sees everyone else clearly. For Hegel, contradiction can never be eliminated at all. History ends not because contradiction disappears, but because we arrive at the recognition that it never will. Kojève's end of history, in other words, is close to the exact opposite of Hegel's own.

**Where Hegel's own philosophy failed him.** None of this excuses what Hegel actually wrote elsewhere, and it would be dishonest to skip past it. In the [_Philosophy of Spirit_](https://www.librarything.com/work/9190/t/Hegel-Philosophy-of-Mind), part of his _Encyclopedia_, Hegel makes plainly racist claims about Africans and Native Americans that cannot be waved away as an unremarkable quirk of his era. McGowan's response is not to minimize this but to locate precisely where the failure occurs. Hegel's own system insists that subjectivity is absolute — that no one exists outside it, and that no group possesses either a hidden natural wisdom or a natural inferiority. The master-servant material discussed [here](/posts/9-freedom-as-self-determination), which scholars including [Susan Buck-Morss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Buck-Morss) connect directly to Hegel's engagement with the Haitian Revolution, already shows the enslaved person moving _closer_ to genuine self-consciousness than the master who dominates him. When Hegel writes about Africans as though they exist outside subjectivity altogether, he is not applying his own system, he is abandoning it, falling back on the unexamined European sources he had been reading, at precisely the point his own logic should have stopped him cold. The same Hegel who refused to grant Egyptian art some mysterious, extra-rational meaning, and who mounted one of the sharpest attacks on record against phrenology — the pseudoscience of reading a person's character from the shape of their skull — for treating people as natural objects rather than divided subjects, betrays that very commitment when he writes about Africa. The lapse is real, and it should not be softened. It is also, on the evidence of his own philosophy, a lapse rather than a conclusion the system actually supports.

**Why history still doesn't feel finished.** Why, then, does history not _feel_ over, more than two centuries after the French Revolution supposedly delivered the relevant recognition to the modern world? McGowan's answer is that confronting freedom — confronting the absence of any consistent authority left to submit to — has produced, in practice, a kind of anxious flight rather than calm acceptance. Two patterns consistently emerge. **Naturalism** installs the gene, in the manner of Richard Dawkins's [_The Selfish Gene_](https://www.librarything.com/work/23538/t/The-Selfish-Gene), as a new self-identical authority that "knows what it wants," letting modern subjects submit to their own biology exactly as earlier subjects once submitted to the divine — the scientific framing, if anything, makes the submission feel more justified rather than less, since it can be dressed up as simply following the facts. **Fundamentalism** appeals more openly to God, nation, or ethnicity as an undivided authority, and because the modern world structurally denies any authority the chance to remain undivided for long, fundamentalist movements get pushed toward increasingly extreme acts, constantly needing to prove that the authority they've staked everything on is still real — when a truly substantial authority, by definition, would never need that kind of proof in the first place. Neither of these is history returning to an earlier stage, a step backward in the story. Both are anxious reactions to history having already arrived exactly where Hegel said it would.
